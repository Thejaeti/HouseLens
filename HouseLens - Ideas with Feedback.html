HouseLens - Ideas with Feedback

First Things First: Making the App Reliably Accessible

The problem today: The app currently runs through Expo Go, which is a developer testing tool — not a real distribution method. It's fragile by design: it can break when Expo Go updates, it requires opening a specific link that may expire, and it sometimes just doesn't load. This is why access has been inconsistent — it worked a time or two but not reliably.

What needs to happen: We need to build a standalone app and install it on the phone directly. There are two paths depending on iPhone or Android:

If on iPhone

  1. Apple Developer Account ($99/year). This is non-negotiable for distributing to iPhones outside the App Store. One account covers unlimited apps and up to 100 test devices.
  2. Use EAS Build to create a real app file. We run one command and Expo's servers build a proper iPhone app (an .ipa file). No Xcode or Mac needed for the build itself.
  3. Register the device. We add the iPhone's unique ID (UDID) to the developer account. This is a one-time step.
  4. Install via ad-hoc distribution. A link is sent, tap it, and the app installs on the home screen like any other app. No Expo Go. No special link to remember. It just lives on the phone.

Alternatively, publishing to the App Store (free to submit with the developer account) means anyone can download it — no device registration needed, no limit on users. But ad-hoc is faster for getting started with a small group of testers.

If on Android

  1. Use EAS Build to create an APK file. Again, one command. No Android Studio needed.
  2. Send the APK directly. Open it on the phone, allow "install from unknown sources" once, and it installs. Done.
  3. Even easier alternative: A Google Play Developer account ($25 one-time) lets us publish to the Play Store's internal testing track — install it from the Play Store directly.

What this solves: Once the standalone app is on the phone, no one needs a laptop open, nothing needs to be running, and nothing expires. Open the app from the home screen and it just works. Updates get pushed automatically.

This is something we can do right now — before building any new features. It's the right first step so the app can actually be used and tested going forward.

Ideas

Hardest What we have now is "point camera, identify house, show a card." This is possible in React Native, but it's genuinely hard, performance-intensive, and the kind of thing large teams at Google and Apple struggle with. I'd call this aspirational — something to aim for long-term, not a near-term build.

Questions

  • How could this interplay with realtors and listings?
Several ways: a "Connect with an agent" button after a property lookup (agents pay referral fees for leads — $500+ per closed deal). Agents or brokerages could pay to be featured when users look up houses in their territory. The app could also surface listing agent contact info directly from the property data. This is both a feature and a monetization path.
  • How does this work with condos or many unit apartments? Or in cities in general?
This is a real challenge. In a building with 200 units, the camera sees one building but there could be many listings inside it. There's no way to distinguish Unit 4B from Unit 12A by pointing a camera at the exterior. The solution would be a hybrid UX: when the app detects a multi-unit building, it shows a list view — "12 units in this building" — and lets you browse or filter. Dense urban areas also make GPS-based house identification less precise, so the app would lean more on address search than camera in those contexts.
  • What other improvements to the process would increase the buyer's experience?
A few ideas: Commute time estimates (how long from this house to your workplace?), price history charts (has this house been going up or down in value?), comparable sales (what did similar nearby houses sell for recently?), and mortgage calculator (what would my monthly payment be at current rates?). Also: the ability to share a house with someone — couples house-hunting together could send properties back and forth within the app.

Follow-Up: AR Deep Dive — What Would It Actually Take?

Good news first: the AR frameworks themselves are free. Apple's ARKit (for iPhones) and Google's ARCore (for Android) cost nothing to use. So there's no licensing fee or API cost for AR itself.

The cost and difficulty are almost entirely in development complexity. Here's why:

The Core Technical Challenge

For AR info to float over a specific house as you look at it, the app needs to solve three problems simultaneously:

  1. Know exactly where you are. GPS on phones is accurate to about 3–5 meters (~10–16 feet). On a suburban street, that margin of error could put you at the wrong house. AR needs much more precision than that.
  2. Know exactly where the house is. We have street addresses, but translating "123 Main St" into a precise 3D point in space that the camera can anchor to is a hard problem.
  3. Track the building visually in real-time. As you move your phone around, the AR overlay needs to stay stuck to the building — not drift, wobble, or jump to the neighbor's house. This requires real-time visual processing that's computationally expensive.

What Exists Today

  • ARKit/ARCore are great at detecting flat surfaces (tables, floors, walls) and placing virtual objects on them. They're not designed to recognize "that's a house and here are its boundaries."
  • Google's Geospatial API (part of ARCore) is the closest thing to what we'd need. It uses Google Street View data to precisely anchor AR content to real-world locations. It's free up to a usage limit. However, it works best in areas with good Street View coverage, and precision varies.
  • LiDAR (on iPhone Pro models) helps with depth sensing and could improve accuracy, but it only works at close range (~5 meters) and limits the audience to premium devices.

Follow-Up: Listing Data, Platform Links & Monetization

This is a great instinct. The idea of showing where a house is listed and letting the user choose where to view it is both a strong feature and a clear monetization path.

Where Does Listing Data Come From?

All home listings ultimately flow from the MLS (Multiple Listing Service) — a shared database that real estate agents use. Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com, and others all pull from MLS data, then add their own extras (Zestimates, reviews, etc.).

To show "this house is listed on Zillow, Redfin, and Realtor.com," we have a few options:

  • Deep links to each platform. Given an address, we can construct URLs that go directly to that property on Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com, etc. This is the simplest approach and doesn't require any API access — we just link out. The user taps, picks their preferred platform, and views the listing there. We can do this now
  • Real estate data APIs. Services like ATTOM, RealtyMole, or RentCast provide property data (beds, baths, price, listing status, listing broker) via API. This lets us show the data inside the app rather than just linking out. Cost: free tier for hobby use, $50–300+/mo for heavier use.
  • Direct MLS access. This would give us the richest data, but MLS access typically requires being a licensed brokerage or partnering with one. Not realistic for a hobby project, but worth knowing about if it ever got serious.

How the UX Could Work

When you look up a house, the app shows a property card with basic info. Below that:

  • A "View Listing On:" section with tappable logos — Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com, etc.
  • Each logo is a deep link that opens that property directly on that platform (in their app if installed, or their website).
  • If the house is for sale, the listing platform logos are highlighted and prominent.
  • If not for sale, the links still work (Zillow and Redfin show info on off-market homes too) but are styled more subtly.

The Monetization Angle

This is where it gets interesting. There are several revenue models here, in order of how realistic they are:

  1. Affiliate / referral traffic. Some platforms have affiliate or partner programs that pay when you send them users who then take actions (contact an agent, schedule a tour, etc.). Zillow has a Premier Agent program, and Realtor.com has partnerships. If HouseLens is driving meaningful traffic, these become real conversations.
  2. Paid placement / "featured" logos. If the app had a user base, real estate platforms or local brokerages could pay to have their logo appear first or more prominently in the "View Listing On" section. Think of it like Google search ads — organic results are free, but the top spot is paid.
  3. Lead generation for agents. A "Connect with a local agent" button alongside the platform logos. Agents pay handsomely for qualified leads ($20–50+ per lead, and much more per closed deal). Services like OJO Labs and HomeLight built entire businesses on this model.
  4. Direct platform partnerships. If the app reached meaningful scale, Zillow or Redfin might pay to be the default or exclusive listing partner. This is a longer-term play but it's the kind of deal that could be significant.

What we could build right now for free: The multi-platform deep linking with logos. No API costs, no partnerships needed. Just a clean UX that sends users to their preferred platform. That alone makes the app more useful, and it's the foundation all of the monetization options above would build on.

Step 2: Add tabbed navigation + saved houses list + map of saved houses. These don't require paid APIs, and they transform the app from a single-trick demo into something that feels like a real product.
